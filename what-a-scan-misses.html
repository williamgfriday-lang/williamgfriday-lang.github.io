What a machine scan catches — and what it misses

A machine scan is a good first look at a website. It is not a verdict, and it is not proof your site works for a blind customer. We run machine scans ourselves — they're how we start. But we'd rather tell you plainly what a scan can and can't do than sell you a promise a robot can't keep.

Don't take our word for that. Take the industry's.

Even the biggest names say it now

accessiBe — one of the largest automated accessibility companies in the world — answers this question on its own homepage:

"Can AI alone make my website accessible?" — "No."

Their own site also concedes that customers can still be sued. And their published Excluded Issues list names the things their automated product does not cover:

  • PDFs
  • CAPTCHA
  • Payment iframes — the checkout path
  • SPAs (single-page applications)

Read that list again. Those aren't edge cases. The checkout is where your money moves. Those are the highest-risk paths on a site — and the machine steps around them.

The Federal Trade Commission has weighed in, too. In April 2025, the FTC's final order against accessiBe — with a $1 million payment — barred the company from claiming its automated products make a site compliant with accessibility standards without competent evidence. The regulator's position is simple: a machine scan, by itself, is not evidence.

UserWay, the largest player of all, sells its human layer separately — starting at a $4,900 audit. Their self-serve product is machine-only. Even at their scale, the human check is a different, expensive thing — because it is a different thing.

What a machine scan catches

A scan is genuinely good at the mechanical layer:

  • Images with no text alternative
  • Buttons and links with no accessible name
  • Form fields with no label
  • Color contrast failures
  • Missing page structure — headings, landmarks, language tags
  • Keyboard traps a script can detect

That list matters. It is the right first step, and it's why our first step is free.

What a machine scan misses

A scan cannot tell you whether a blind person can actually use your site:

  • Whether the checkout can be completed with a screen reader, start to finish
  • Whether a CAPTCHA locks a real customer out entirely
  • Whether your PDF menu, application, or price list can be read at all
  • Whether a pop-up, menu, or booking flow makes sense when you can't see it — or traps the keyboard in a loop
  • Whether the error message on your form is announced, or silent
  • Whether the "accessible" widget on the page actually helps — or just sits on top of barriers it never touched

A scan reads code. A person lives the experience. The lawsuits are filed over the experience.
